  • Host: Today's article was a dark one: Jane Toppan, a nurse in the late 1800s who killed dozens of her patients. Honestly, it gives me chills.  
  • Guest: Same here. What got me was the nickname, "Jolly Jane." Everyone thought she was this warm, cheerful person, and she was hiding in plain sight the whole time.  
  • Host: Right. And she took advantage of the one thing a nurse really has: trust. Families let her into their homes because doctors kept sending her there.  
  • Guest: She had a great track record, at least on paper. Nobody imagined that a friendly nurse could be the danger.  
  • Host: But were there red flags? The article mentions that some of her coworkers noticed she was strangely interested in death.  
  • Guest: Maybe, but back then that probably just seemed odd, not criminal.  
  • Host: Fair point. The part I really can't wrap my head around is her foster sister, Elizabeth. They grew up together!  
  • Guest: And then she poisoned Elizabeth's husband and nursed him back to health so he'd want to marry her? It's like something out of a horror movie.  
  • Host: I guess deep down she didn't see people as people. They were just experiments to her.  
  • Guest: So how did she finally get caught?  
  • Host: The Davis family. Several of them died within a short time, and their relatives finally put two and two together. They pushed the police to look into it, and the tests found poison.  
  • Guest: And from there, investigators started to piece together her other cases. It makes you wonder how many deaths were never connected to her.  
  • Host: Exactly. How did she get away with it for so long?  
  • Guest: Partly the time period, I think. In hindsight, the signs seem obvious, but there were no modern lab tests back then.  
  • Host: I should point out the ending, too, because a lot of people misunderstand it. "Not guilty by reason of insanity" doesn't mean she walked free.  
  • Guest: No, she spent the rest of her life in a state hospital. She died there in 1938.  
  • Host: So was she really ill, or was she clever enough to fool the doctors?  
  • Guest: I honestly don't know. People who do terrible things like this are hard to make sense of. Three psychiatrists said she was ill, and she confessed to thirty-one murders without any regret. That doesn't sound like a healthy mind.  
  • Host: On the other hand, she planned things carefully for years. That takes a lot of control.  
  • Guest: That's the puzzle. Either way, the real story is the people she hurt and the families who trusted her.  
  • Host: Well said. Maybe the lesson is that trust matters, but it shouldn't be blind. Definitely food for thought. Thanks for joining me.  
  • Guest: Thanks for having me.
