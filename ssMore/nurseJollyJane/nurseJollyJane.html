Jolly Jane: The Nurse Who Loved to Watch Her Patients Die

▶
Exercises, Podcast, Music Videos (go to bottom)
Tap or hover the marked word/phrase to see what it means.

She was cheerful, friendly, and highly recommended by doctors. Her patients trusted her with their lives. Unfortunately, that was exactly what made her so dangerous.

In the late 1800s, doctors in Massachusetts regularly recommended a private nurse named Jane Toppan to wealthy families. Known as "Jolly Jane," she had a warm personality and seemed to enjoy caring for the sick and elderly. But behind her friendly smile was a terrifying secret. She was killing her patients with poison. She wasn't doing it for money, jealousy, or revenge; she was doing it just for the thrill.

Jane started her nursing career after training as a nurse at Cambridge Hospital. Her cheerful personality earned her the nickname "Jolly Jane," and she became popular with many patients. However, some of her colleagues noticed that she had a strange fascination with death and autopsies.

A Deadly Experiment

While working at the hospital, Jane began experimenting on her patients with morphine and atropine, two powerful drugs. Using different doses, she became fascinated by her patients' reactions to the drugs.

At some point, she began using this deadly combination of drugs for murder. Jane later admitted that she enjoyed watching people die. She sometimes climbed into bed with them, holding and caressing them as they slowly died.

After leaving hospital work, Jane became a private nurse, looking after wealthy families. Doctors trusted and recommended her, and she had a good reputation as a skilled caregiver.

Friends, Family, and Victims

Jane's victims were not limited to strangers. She also poisoned friends, acquaintances, and even people who had once been kind to her.

One of her most disturbing crimes was the murder of her foster sister, Elizabeth, with whom she had grown up. Jane wanted to marry Elizabeth's husband, Oramel, so she poisoned her. Jane later confessed that as she held Elizabeth, she enjoyed watching her die in her arms.

Her plan to marry Oramel failed. She even tried poisoning him and then nursing him back to health, hoping to make him grateful enough to marry her. But in the end, he ordered her out of his home.

The Family That Exposed Her

In 1901, Jane was caring for the Davis family. Several family members died after sudden illnesses. At first, their deaths seemed unrelated and were blamed on natural causes.

With so many family members dying in a short time, relatives became suspicious. They demanded an investigation, and the exhumed bodies were found with traces of poison.

Investigators began examining other suspicious deaths connected to Jane, and on October 29, 1901, police arrested her.

A Confession and a Lifetime Behind Bars

During her 1902 murder trial, Jane admitted to killing 31 people. She showed no remorse for her victims.

Three psychiatrists testified that Jane was mentally ill, and the jury eventually found her "not guilty by reason of insanity". Instead of going free, however, Jane was committed to Taunton State Hospital, a psychiatric institution for the criminally insane. She remained there for the rest of her life, dying in 1938.

Was Jane genuinely insane, or had she discovered a way to escape the death penalty? Even today, that question remains part of the mystery surrounding Jolly Jane.

Legal Note:

Not guilty by reason of insanity: Does not mean the person is set free. It means that the person committed the act, but because of mental illness, they were not in control of their actions or didn't clearly understand right from wrong. Usually, they are committed to a psychiatric facility until they no longer pose a danger to society.

Comprehension Questions

with suggested answers

Why was Jane Toppan called "Jolly Jane"?
Because she had a cheerful, warm, and friendly personality.
What strange interest did some of Jane's colleagues notice?
They noticed she had a strange fascination with death and autopsies.
Why did Jane kill her patients?
Not for money, jealousy, or revenge. She did it just for the thrill, and she enjoyed watching people die.
Why did wealthy families hire Jane as a private nurse?
Doctors trusted and recommended her, and she had a good reputation as a skilled caregiver.
Why did Jane poison her foster sister, Elizabeth?
She wanted to marry Elizabeth's husband, Oramel.
How did Jane try to make Oramel marry her, and what happened?
She poisoned him and then nursed him back to health, hoping he would be grateful enough to marry her. In the end, he ordered her out of his home.
Why did the Davis family's relatives become suspicious?
So many family members died after sudden illnesses in a short time.
What did investigators find when the bodies were exhumed?
They found traces of poison.
How many people did Jane admit to killing?
She admitted to killing 31 people, and she showed no remorse.
What was the jury's decision, and what happened to Jane afterward?
The jury found her "not guilty by reason of insanity." She was committed to a psychiatric institution, where she stayed until she died in 1938.

Discussion Questions

  • Do you think Jane was genuinely insane, or did she find a way to escape the death penalty? Why?
  • Why do you think so many people trusted Jane for so long? How can families make sure the people caring for their loved ones are safe?
